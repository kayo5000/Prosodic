I'm applying for the Machine Learning Researcher role on Apple Music's
recommender systems team, requisition 200665642-2114.

I write and record rap, and I build software. Plenty of people can train a model
better than I can today. Not many of them can hear a verse and tell you why the
third bar lands late on purpose. I've spent the last year turning that ear into
something a machine can actually run, and the combination is what I'm offering

The engine I built reads lyrics as phonemes, syllables, stress, and placement
against the beat. The code wasn't the hard part. The hard part was making calls
with nobody around to check them. Pronunciation dictionaries miss most of the
rhymes that really land in a verse, and the fast fix is to loosen the matching
threshold, which inflates your numbers and quietly ruins them. I took the slow
route and modeled how the words get performed instead, so the matcher never had
to get looser. I make that trade the same way every time. I'd rather hold a
smaller number I trust than a bigger one I can't defend in front of somebody who
knows the material.

The other line I hold is that the system describes and never judges. An artist
sitting behind the beat isn't making a mistake, they're doing something on
purpose, and software that calls that an error is software no artist keeps open
twice. What a measurement is allowed to say about a person is a design question
before it's a technical one, and those are the questions I want to be working
on.

I did this alone, while finishing a B.S. in Software Engineering at WGU, and
before that I spent eight years in the Army, where the work got done whether or
not anyone was watching. I start an M.S. with an AI engineering specialization
next.

What I don't have is scale, or people who will tell me I'm wrong. When I found
out an idea of mine already had a name in music information retrieval, that was
better news than if it had been original. I want to be somewhere that happens
regularly, and I'd like to talk about what your team is building.
